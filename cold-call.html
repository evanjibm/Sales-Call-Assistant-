What a good cold call is for

A cold call is about creating curiosity and earning a next step. That's what makes the process repeatable: you stay calm, you sound confident, and you know what success looks like. The goal is to learn enough about a prospect's situation to determine whether a follow-up conversation makes sense.

If you leave the call with new information, genuine interest, or a clear next step, it's a win.

2 Discover: three MEDDPICC questions ask in order

Objective: find out whether the problem is real for them, what it costs, and who owns it, enough to know a longer conversation is worth both people's time.

A cold call tests relevance; it does not complete qualification. Uncover a potential problem, its impact, and ownership. Capture other qualification insights when offered, but don't force a full MEDDPICC interview into the first call.

1
Is the problem real?
Implicate the Pain

"Most I talk to at are dealing with ... How is that showing up, if at all, within your team?"

Giving them an easy way out is the point. It signals you're testing an idea rather than fishing, and it makes a yes mean something.

Strong: they correct you, add detail, or say "well, sort of, for us it's more..."
Weak: "Yeah, sure." That's politeness, not agreement. Don't count it.
2
What does it cost them?
Pain + Metrics

"What does that actually cost you today: is it , or is it something else?"

Easy to answer because you gave them the options. You get the shape of a metric without asking them to price anything.

Strong: any number, any unit. "About two days a week." "We missed the quarter by 8%."
Weak: "Hard to say." Fine, move on. Don't push a stranger for a number.
3
Do they own it?
Champion

"Is something you own day to day, or does it sit with someone else on the team?"

Sounds like good manners; works as qualification. Skip it entirely if the first two already earned you the meeting.

Strong: "That's me," or "that's me and my director."
Weak: "That's really a thing." Ask for the name and the intro instead of the meeting.
Data check: how long to spend here2
About 5 min 50 sec

is how long a successful cold call runs, with the seller talking roughly 55% of the time. Three questions is the ceiling. The eleven-to-fourteen-question benchmark is a discovery call, where the customer has already agreed to be asked things. On a cold call they've agreed to nothing, and every question is a small favor.

Five questions not to ask on a cold call

Every one is a good MEDDPICC question later in a deal. Here, each tells the person they've been entered into an evaluation they never agreed to.

Don't askWhy it costs you the callSay this instead
"Do you have budget for this?"There's nothing to budget for yet.Nothing. Budget is a discovery-call question. There's no cold-call version of it.
"Are you the decision maker?"It either offends them or gets a defensive yes."Is something you own day to day, or does it sit with someone else?"
"Who else would be involved in evaluating this?"Assumes an evaluation. Invites "we're not evaluating anything."Ask it after they say yes, as logistics: "Anyone on your side you'd want on the call?"

A call that never books a meeting can still be a win. What they use today, who owns the initiative, what it costs, or where they are in the buying cycle all make your second call into that account better than your first.

3 Explore: navigate objections tap an objection

Objective: normalize the objection, find out what's behind it, and ask one more question, without getting defensive. Confidence reads as competence, and confidence comes from repetition.

1 · Acknowledge

Take the pressure off so they don't have to defend the objection. It's not a rebuttal; it tells them you've heard this before and it didn't end the conversation.

2 · Explore

One question to find out what's underneath. "Can you tell me more?" · "What's driving that today?" Ask it, then stop talking.

3 · Continue

Point your next question at what a cold call can get: is the problem real (Pain), what it costs (Metrics), or who owns it (Champion). Names, numbers, or incumbents along the way? Write them down; never chase.

Two things that break the move

Normalize once. Saying "a lot of my customers..." twice in one call stops sounding like experience and starts sounding like a script.

Don't solve the objection on the phone. "We don't have budget" isn't an invitation to justify the spend; it's an invitation to find out what they're spending on instead.

For anything you haven't heard before: don't rush to rebut. Acknowledge, ask a follow-up, and look for the underlying reason. Most objections reveal useful information if you're willing to explore them.

Data check: objections where we retreat too quickly3
  • "It's not me" / "I'm not the right person" → ask who it is. If they can't name a person, ask which team or function.
  • "I've left the company" → ask who took over. They know, they're not protective of it, and they usually feel a little bad about the mix-up.

4 Secure the next step two of three, and you ask

Objective: earn a next step that matches what the conversation revealed: a meeting when there's relevance and interest; otherwise a referral, useful follow-up, future reconnect, or a respectful qualify-out.

Signal 01 · They named it

They put the problem in their own words, an actual sentence, not "yeah, sure." If they corrected your hypothesis, that counts.

Signal 02 · They own it

They said "we" or "I" about the problem, or confirmed it sits with them. Another team's problem is a referral, not a meeting, still valuable.

Signal 03 · They leaned in

They asked you a question back, or answered at more length than the question deserved. You can't manufacture this one, and it's the best predictor the meeting happens.

The threshold: when to ask4
Two of three, and you ask

Two strong signals are usually enough to propose a next step. Don't extend the call to complete a checklist; when relevance and interest are clear, preserve the momentum and ask.

All three signals · just book it

"Then let's get thirty minutes. I'll walk you through how got their [their words, not yours] down, and what it took. Thursday at 2, or Friday morning?"

Their language, one proof point, two specific times. Nothing left to decide except which slot.

Two of three · sell the conversation first

"Does it make sense for me to show you how handled that?" → "Great, do you have your calendar handy?"

Asking for their interest is a smaller ask than their time, so lead with it. Once they say yes to the idea, the calendar is a formality, but open it while you're on the phone.

One or none · take something smaller

"Fair enough. Can I send you the one-pager on what did with , and come back to you after [their event / quarter end]?"

Forcing a meeting here gets a no-show. A disproved hypothesis is still a real result. If they ask to be left alone, close it cleanly; a clean qualify-out is a good outcome, not a failed call.

Before you hang up, and right after

Get it in the calendar on the call. "Do you have your calendar in front of you?" A verbal yes with no invite is the single biggest source of meetings that never happen. Send it while you're still on the phone and confirm they have it.

Then write down five things: their sentence about the problem, word for word · any number they gave · any name they dropped · anything they use today · what you deliberately didn't ask. That last one is your discovery agenda, already written.
